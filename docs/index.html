Methodology & data notes

Forecast signal

For each area the single worst qualifying slot is shown: rainy-season trimesters within the forecast horizon, drought side only (forecast percentile < 50), with at least moderate skill (r ≥ 0.30). The return period is directional: a 10-yr value means a forecast this dry occurs about once a decade in the 1981–present hindcast for that area and season. The Valid season selector defaults to the worst drought among forecast seasons; "incl. in-season" also scans trimesters already in progress (the worst signal is often a season underway), and picking a trimester (e.g. MJJ) shows that season's forecast for every area. The trimester actually used is shown as its compact code (e.g. MJJ) — inside scatter bubbles where space allows, centred on each area when a country is selected on the map, and bold in every tooltip. Forecast data are detrended, matching the Map tab's defaults.

Caseloads (PiN / targeted)

PiN / Targeted come from each plan's latest reference period via ds-hnrp-mirror; figures published at admin-2/3 are summed to admin-1 (some plans publish subnational needs only at admin-3 — DR Congo, Myanmar; where such a plan reports a single "Final HRP caseload" instead of Intersectoral, that is shown as the Intersectoral figure). Caseloads appear only for countries whose newest plan cycle is this year or last: Ethiopia (last subnational needs 2024, no newer plan) and Syria (last needs 2024; its 2025 severity analysis publishes no population figures) are absent entirely. PiN is the plan's headline total for the intersectoral caseload — not a severity band (it has no JIAF-class or IPC-phase breakdown). All PiN/targeted figures shown are the plans' intersectoral ones. Each country shows its latest plan cycle (year in the country selector, the Plan column, and tooltips); where no newer plan exists the previous cycle is shown (e.g. Guatemala, latest 2025). Where the current cycle publishes a unit's PiN but no targeted figure (or omits a unit entirely), the newest earlier cycle fills the gap — a stale targeted is flagged with its year (* in the table, "(2024 plan)" in tooltips), and a wholly carried-over unit shows the older year in its Plan column. A country can still be absent from a given caseload when its plan never publishes that sector subnationally — the columns then show dashes.

Severity sources

The Severity row picks the source and level. IPC/CH acute food insecurity phases (default, via ds-ipc-mirror), JIAF severity from the HNRP severity analysis (latest analysis year per country), or the plan's PiN itself (no severity level; shares then divide by the plan's JIAF analysed population). JIAF figures are people-level: the plan's PiN-by-Severity distribution (JIAF 2.0 PbS workbooks, latest analysis cycle — mostly 2026), i.e. the number of people in need at each intersectoral severity class per area ("JIAF PiN 3+" = PiN at classes 3–5). Note the granularity: the PbS assigns each finest analysis unit (× population group) one class and places that unit's whole PiN there — within-unit splits exist only where plans publish them (Yemen and Syria, at most two classes). So at admin-2 most units show a single class (matching the unit's area classification), and a mixed distribution emerges only where sub-units or population groups sit at different classes — admin-1 bars mix their districts; IPC, by contrast, publishes genuine within-area phase distributions. Some plans publish PiN without a usable class breakdown (Guatemala, El Salvador and Venezuela publish no classes; South Sudan fills one constant class on every unit — a template artifact) — there, each unit's PiN is placed at its area classification from the severity analysis, the same one-class-per-unit semantic the PbS encodes everywhere else (flagged in the bar-chart title). IPC and JIAF figures do not line up: they measure different things (acute food insecurity vs inter-sectoral needs) over different analysed populations, scopes, and periods — compare shapes, not values. In IPC mode the map, scatter, and table also include IPC-covered areas outside any HNRP (no PiN/severity/targeted; plotted at 0% targeted) — surfacing possible shocks beyond plan scope. Only IPC analyses valid in 2025 or later are included; countries whose series stalled earlier (Ethiopia 2021, Angola/El Salvador 2022, Burkina Faso Aug 2024, Timor-Leste 2024) carry no IPC here.

Admin levels

The Admin level selector switches the whole tab between four views. Lowest available (the default) shows each country at the finest level we can: admin-3 where plans publish it against boundaries we hold (Burkina Faso communes, Myanmar townships, Syria sub-districts, and DR Congo's 519 zones de santé — via the SNIS/OCHA health-zone shapefile, which matches the plan's zone codes exactly), admin-2 for the 21-country admin-2 scope, admin-1 elsewhere. Fixed admin-1/2/3 views show one level only. Admin-2 covers ~4,900 units across 21 countries with admin-2 boundaries and zonal statistics in our database (AFG, BFA, CAF, CMR, COD, COL, GTM, HND, HTI, MLI, MOZ, NER, NGA, SDN, SLV, SOM, SSD, TCD, UKR, VEN, YEM — the rest lack admin-2 polygons on our side and are excluded on that basis; Myanmar publishes admin-1/3 only). No admin-3 forecast exists: admin-3 units inherit their parent admin-2's forecast (skill, return period, rainy season) verbatim — only the humanitarian figures are finer. IPC is published at admin-1/2; units finer than a country's published IPC level inherit the parent analysis, prorated by each unit's population share — tooltips flag these as "downscaled from admin-N", and they are shares of an area-level analysis, not unit-level assessments. Admin-3 population denominators come from WorldPop 2020 (1 km UN-adjusted, summed over the same admin-3 boundaries). Figures published only at admin-1 cannot be downscaled and show dashes at admin-2. Forecast skill at admin-2 is computed identically, per unit; smaller units are noisier, so treat marginal signals with extra caution. The table shows at most 500 rows at a time; admin-2 unit names carry their admin-1 parent in parentheses.

IPC estimate periods

Now uses the most recent analysis covering the forecast issuance month (preferring a current-type analysis); Forecast window uses the most recent projection overlapping the 6-month horizon — and is offered only where it differs from Now. The dropdown states each choice's exercise (analysis) month and validity window — exactly, once a country is selected; as a cross-country range otherwise — and every tooltip names the analysis used for that area.

Reading the scatter

Both axes divide by the same population base: the admin's total population — the UNFPA COD-PS baseline via HDX HAPI where present and trusted, else the plan's own HNO/JIAF baseline total (self-consistent with the PiN/targeted figures; e.g. all of Yemen, which has no COD-PS in HAPI). The tooltip names the source and reference year. Where neither exists, the fallback is the larger of the IPC analysed base and the plan's JIAF analysed base — and shares there can overstate against the true total where the analysis covers only part of the population. A shared denominator makes position compare absolute headcounts: above the 45° line, more people sit in the severity band than the plan targets (a possible coverage gap); below it, targeting exceeds it — and the base does not change when the severity source switches. Axes are fixed 0–100%. Every admin with a displayable forecast plots, regardless of HNRP or IPC membership — areas with no figure for an axis sit at 0%, at the origin if they have neither. The severity bars plot classes 3–5 by default (classes 1–2 dominate populous areas and drown the signal — a checkbox restores them).

How the forecast alerts are calculated

Each area is shaded by where the latest ECMWF SEAS5 seasonal precipitation forecast sits within its own forecast history, but only where the forecast has demonstrated skill against observations. The same method runs at two resolutions — country averages and the native SEAS5 0.4° grid (with ERA5 aggregated to that grid) — switchable with the Country/Pixel toggle. The values below are the defaults used by this map; the full interactive app lets you change every one of them.

1. Data

  • Forecast: ECMWF SEAS5 ensemble-mean precipitation, hindcasts from 1981 to present.
  • Observations / climatology: ERA5 reanalysis precipitation, same areas and period.

2. From a forecast to an anomaly category

  1. Aggregate to the trimester. For a given issue month, the monthly forecast is averaged over the 3-month target season (e.g. a June issue → Jul–Aug–Sep), giving one forecast value per year. ERA5 is aggregated the same way (complete seasons only). For in-season trimesters (issued after the season started, e.g. an August issue → Jul–Aug–Sep) the already-elapsed months are taken from ERA5 observations and only the remaining month(s) from SEAS5 — each forecast month first bias-corrected against ERA5 for that calendar month (mean and spread matched in log space) so observed and forecast months can be blended without the forecast's month-specific bias leaking into the trimester value.
  2. Transform, normalise & detrend. Values are log-transformed, and the forecast is rescaled to match the ERA5 mean and spread, so the two are comparable. A linear trend is then removed from both the forecast and the observations (in log space, keeping each series' mean) — so the forecast is judged against recent conditions rather than the full historical range, and cases where SEAS5 and ERA5 trend in opposite directions are corrected.
  3. Locate the forecast. The forecast's percentile within the historical forecast distribution is computed, along with an empirical (Weibull) return period — how often a forecast this dry (or this wet) has occurred.
  4. Categorise. Below 50th percentile → drought side, above → flood side. The band depends on the return period (see defaults below).

3. Skill

Skill is the temporal Pearson correlation (r) between the normalised, detrended hindcast forecast and ERA5 observations across the full record (1981–present). Areas are only alerted where the forecast is skilful:

  • High skill: r ≥ 0.50 — solid fill.
  • Moderate skill: 0.30 ≤ r < 0.50 — hatched fill.
  • Low skill: r < 0.30 — no alert (cross-hatch / neutral).

There is no single official correlation cut-off: WMO's SVSLRF guidance recommends judging significance locally rather than a fixed value. The defaults are chosen so that 0.30 ≈ the 95% statistical-significance level for a ~40-year hindcast, and 0.50 ≈ "useful" skill (r ≈ 0.5 explains ~25% of variance; IRI/Tippett et al. 2010 note RPSS ≈ 0.1 corresponds to r ≈ 0.44). ECMWF's familiar ACC ≥ 0.6 "useful" mark refers to spatial medium-range skill, a different metric.

Skill also depends on leadtime — how far ahead the forecast is issued. For a trimester, leadtime is the number of months from the issue month to the trimester's first month (a June issue → Jul–Aug–Sep is leadtime 1), and skill generally decays as leadtime grows. SEAS5 runs 6 months ahead, so leadtimes go up to 4 — beyond that a 3-month trimester would extend past the forecast horizon. Negative leadtimes (−1, −2) are in-season issues: the trimester had already started, so its elapsed months are ERA5 observations and only the remainder is forecast. Their skill is computed the same way (blended series vs. the fully-observed trimester) and is naturally much higher, since 1–2 of the 3 months are shared with the observations — read it as confidence in the season's final outcome, not forecast skill per se. The Skill map and Skill by country tabs show r for every trimester and leadtime, coloured at these same cutoffs (with negative r shown as its own category). Pixel-level layers cover the fully-forecast leadtimes only (0–4); in-season estimates are country-level.

4. Rainy-season (in-season) selection

An area–trimester is treated as in season when that trimester carries a meaningful share of the annual rainfall — by default, the trimester mean is ≥ 15% of the annual total (with no additional per-month minimum). By default the map only shows alerts for in-season trimesters; the “Show alerts outside rainy season” toggle reveals the rest.
